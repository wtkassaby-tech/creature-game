# Designing creatures people love: the synthesis

Distilled from 46 research briefs (this folder): Gen 1 to Legends: Z-A, each from four angles (starters and commons, signature designs, legendaries, fan reception), plus six spin-off angles (GO, Mystery Dungeon, Snap, the card game, mobile games, the anime). Principles only. Pokemon names below are evidence, never templates: every ClayPals creature stays original (CLAUDE.md hard rule 2).

**How strong the evidence is.** Gen 1 to 7 rest on real numbers: the 2020 official Pokemon of the Year vote (6.6M+ votes), the r/Pokemon 2019 survey (52,725 votes), Nintendo Life per-generation polls (1,500 to 5,600 votes each), Japanese polls and critic lists. Gen 8, Gen 9, Z-A and most spin-off briefs ran out of search budget and lean on Wikipedia and memory; treat those as hypotheses. All polls are self-selected and skewed by nostalgia: Gen 1 averages about 121 votes per creature against 65 overall, yet the most loved Gen 1 dragon ranked only 18th in a young-skewed 2021 Japanese poll.

---

## 1. The twelve patterns that recur across eras

Counts are how many of the 46 briefs raise the pattern, rounded.

**1. The silhouette reads at icon size (about 42 of 46).** The single most repeated finding. Every top design (the grinning ghost blob, the flame-tailed dragon, the three-headed dragon, the costumed ghost) is recognisable as a flat black shape. Flat or ambiguous shapes (the flat fish, the plain sphere) sink. Z-A's most covered design story was a mocked silhouette (Mega Starmie's legs), seen in a low-resolution leak.

**2. One signature feature carries the identity (about 38).** A grin, a flame tail, scythe arms, glowing rings, a blade horn, a leaf bowtie. Fans name the creature by its feature. Ornament beyond that feature dilutes it: the Mega forms most criticised were already-finished silhouettes with detail added.

**3. A familiar animal or myth anchor plus exactly one twist (about 35).** Fire dog, iron snake, ninja frog, armoured raven, moth-as-sun. The anchor makes it read instantly; the twist makes it ownable. The mirror finding is the strongest anti-pattern in the dataset: an everyday object with a face (section 3).

**4. Cute start, cool payoff (about 30).** The starter lines that win polls grow from a small round baby into a powerful final form, and the payoff drives love for the whole line (the top Gen 1 starter won its type's vote largely on its final form). Gyarados-style weak-to-mighty transformations earn the most emotional credit. But the payoff must keep the charm: lines that lose the baby's appeal at the end (Chikorita's line, Delphox) are named as disappointments.

**5. Personality shows in the face, eyes and pose (about 30).** Cheeky, dopey, vacant or anxious faces become memes and fan art for free (Mudkip, Fuecoco, Wooloo). Gen 9 briefs make the head the personality carrier.

**6. One clear concept a child can say aloud (about 30).** "Disaster omen." "Lonely creature in a homemade costume." "Haunted candelabra." Designs that need a pun to explain them (Exeggutor) or carry two gags (Kyurem) are mocked.

**7. A restrained palette: one main colour, one accent, three or four at most (about 25).** A dark body with one glowing accent is the most successful single recipe (Umbreon won Gen 2 at every poll; Chandelure won Gen 5). Gen 4 and Mega criticism centres on four-plus-colour clutter.

**8. A story or emotion beats stats (about 25).** Mimikyu (3rd overall in 2020, about 99,000 votes) won on a sentence of sadness. Darkrai on sympathetic menace. Mystery Dungeon sold 17M copies on character while scoring 59 to 69 on Metacritic. Equal stats do not mean equal love: the three Johto beasts share a 580 total and split on looks.

**9. Every stage and form adds a new idea (about 22).** Scaled-up copies (Electrode), steps that only gate something (Happiny), blank middle stages (Shelgon, Spewpa, Cosmoem at 0.1%) and featureless transitional forms poll near zero. Middle stages are where love dies: Marshtomp 0%, Combusken 0.2%.

**10. Being met early, used often and seen in the world builds love (about 20).** Bidoof became a meme through its job, Starly through staying useful, Greninja through anime and Smash exposure, Snorlax through blocking a road. Non-starter mascot lines met in mid-game beat starters two to three times (Lucario 102,259 votes).

**11. Love is spread thin, so quality must be roster-wide (about 18).** The top creature of most generations holds only 6 to 15% of its poll. Gen 5 shipped 156 new creatures and placed none in the overall top 10; a tighter roster concentrates affection. Gen 7 shows the opposite shape: one 12% standout and a long tail under 1%.

**12. Rares are special by shape, story and earned effort, never by colour (about 18).** Sets that share a template (the Kanto birds, the Swords of Justice at 0.1 to 0.2%, the forces trio) sink together. The unpaired capstone with a story role earned about four times the votes of each box mascot (Rayquaza 60,939 vs Kyogre 15,585). Rares that lose to commons disappoint.

Two smaller patterns worth keeping: **Sugimori's balance rule** (add one soft or odd detail to a cool design, one tough detail to a cute one; about 12 briefs) and **one deliberate imperfection** (a freckle, a mismatched ear), which in clay reads as handmade charm.

## 2. How taste changed, and what still works

| Era | What won | What it teaches now |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gen 1 | Simple animal archetypes, chunky shapes built for tiny sprites | Simplicity survives every era; first exposure is the biggest popularity multiplier |
| Gen 2 | Sleek dark designs with one glow; babies with their own personality | A new type deserves its own visual language; babies must not be shrunk copies |
| Gen 3 | A deliberate turn to cool and monstrous; humanoids accepted | Playtesters said these "don't look like Pokemon" and they became favourites: do not over-trust first reactions to a bold shape |
| Gen 4 | Earned pseudo-mascots; cross-gen evolutions that filled gaps | 14 legendaries diluted specialness; mascots outpolled every legendary |
| Gen 5 | Angular cool; strong animal-plus-concept designs | Object designs and an oversized roster were punished; going all-angular cost warmth |
| Gen 6 | Rounded, big-eyed cute; designs tied to mechanics | A creature tied to a system (affection, stance change) gains a reason to bond; ornament on finished shapes fails |
| Gen 7 | Story-first designs; habitat reinterpretations of known creatures | Variants with a lore reason are cheap and loved; alien, faceless designs split players |
| Gen 8 | Plain commons with vulnerable personalities; real-world hooks | A simple round common can become the mascot; never remove owned creatures (the generation's loudest backlash) |
| Gen 9 | Expressive, memeable faces; whole-line promise | Fans fear a worse final form, so show where a line goes early |
| Z-A | Exaggerate one trait of a loved design | Broken proportions and gated access colour reception |

**The direction of travel.** Early eras rewarded pure shape. Later eras increasingly reward story, emotion, behaviour and use: Snap shows creatures loved for what they do, Sleep built 30M downloads on caring for one sleepy creature, the anime made flawed, disobedient partners the favourites. **What still works now:** a chunky readable silhouette, one feature, one concept, a face with personality, a reason to bond. That is exactly the clay-toy brief.

## 3. Anti-patterns in disliked designs

1. **Object with a face.** The most mocked pattern in every era: the ball, the trash bag, the ice cream cone, the gears, the keyring, the compass nose, the sideways heart. Objects succeed only when given living-creature logic and a mood (the candelabra, the sword and shield).
2. **Formless blobs** with no anatomy (the sludges, the gas ball, the jelly).
3. **Pun mash-ups** that need a sentence of explanation, or two gags merged.
4. **Scaled-up copies and pointless stages** that add a step but no idea.
5. **Template sets**: trios or families that differ only by colour.
6. **Uncanny human mimicry** (clown, human-faced designs) and humanised animal evolutions that lose the base idea.
7. **Clutter**: too many colours, ridges, tassels, spots or concepts.
8. **Grim, gross or accidental readings**: depressing lore, innuendo in descriptions, an unintended phallic shape.
9. **Broken proportions** on a familiar silhouette.
10. **Gimmick quantity**: 28 near-identical forms, random spots that read as a mess.
11. **Plain real animals with no hook** (pest-like mongoose, plain fish, generic grub).
12. **Losing the charm at the final stage.**
13. **Mascot clones** that read as a copy of a more famous design.
14. **Rarity that does not match usefulness, or is locked away** behind an event few could reach, an obscure puzzle, or a paid or ranked gate.

## 4. Guidance by role

**Starters.** Offer three personalities, not three power ranks: one cute, one cool, one odd. Expect a near three-way split (the Galar poll went 38/37/25). Make the base form appealing enough to be picked and the final form the payoff, with one visible element carried from baby to adult. Preview the final silhouette early so players trust the line. Let a short personality choice pick or suggest the starter so it feels like a reflection of the player.

**Early commons.** The most-met creatures set the emotional hook and get the least poll love unless designed as characters. Each needs one memorable trait (a tail, a stance, a behaviour), a round chunky body, a distinct sound and a job. Include one workhorse that stays useful late and one humble, slightly goofy creature that can become a community joke. Put the best-designed creatures in the first biomes: early exposure is the largest popularity multiplier in the data.

**Signature mid-game creatures.** This is where the fan favourites live in Gen 2, 4 and 5. Give each biome one pseudo-mascot line with a long, earned growth arc and one hero part. Plan a few sleek dark designs with a single glow on purpose; they top polls in nearly every era. Give at least some mid-game species a two-way branch so players pick a favourite.

**Giants.** Read as a force of nature through a few huge shapes, one elemental idea per giant, and a silhouette class unlike any common. Give each a story role in its biome (guardian, blocker, the thing that went wrong), a staged reveal, music and a signature animation; critics repeatedly cite these for big creatures. Pair menace with sympathy. Keep patterns few on big bodies: busy detail at scale reads goofy.

**Legendaries.** One-line identity, original mythic grounding, a thumbnail-readable silhouette. If paired, make them contrasting mirrors (warm and cool, heavy and light, even contrasting name sounds). Never a colour-swapped trio. Match their power to their rarity. Make access a quest with a lore reason, not a chore or an obscure puzzle. Keep the tier scarce.

**Mythics (the Firstling).** Love here comes from the secret, the chase and the discovery, more than from size: a hidden creature added quietly drove a famous sales boost, and small charming mythics beat imposing ones in several polls. Earned in play by every player, never paid, never locked to an event few can reach. Give it a story spine and a gameplay reason to matter. A shared visual mark (a face, a glaze or a sculpt signature) echoed across the giants and legendaries makes the rare tier read as one family descended from it.

## 5. Guidance by biome

Each biome gets its own palette, clay finish and motif so categories feel distinct (Gen 2 lesson), and two or three shared marking motifs so its creatures look like a set (Gen 3 lesson).

- **Leaf forest.** First impressions live here, so the strongest, roundest, friendliest designs go here. Animal-plus-plant is a proven combination. Matte greens with one warm accent. Avoid a plant with a face and no animal body.
- **Sand canyon.** Function-from-shape: digging claws, armour plates, burrowing bodies. Ochre and terracotta with one cool accent. Avoid plain rock primitives and single-feature concepts; give rocks a face and behaviour (sunbathing, burrowing).
- **Water coast.** Graceful, rideable shapes and chubby, cheeky shore creatures both work. Glossy finish, one fin or gill feature. Plain fish are the most-cited filler; every fish needs a twist.
- **Toxic swamp.** The highest-risk biome: sludges and gas balls are the most disliked shapes in Gen 1. Give every toxic creature real anatomy, a clear face and a sympathetic behaviour. Ugly-cute is allowed sparingly, never gross-out. Glossy purple-green with one bright warning accent.
- **Frost.** Fluffy, round, huggable bodies with ice crystals as the single accent. Avoid "icicle on icicles" and dessert objects. Good home for habitat reinterpretations of creatures from warmer biomes.
- **Crystal caves.** Gems as the glowing accent on an animal body; glitter finish. Geometric mineral designs without a face or asymmetry are the polarising failure. Creatures that light up in response to the player feel alive (Snap lesson).
- **Fire volcanic.** Flame belongs to the body (a tail, a collar, a back) as the one accent. Dark body, bright glow, never a wall of red. Concept-first lava creatures are fine only with a creature body.
- **Storm sky.** Birds and serpents, with jagged, distinct silhouettes. The bird trio template is the cautionary tale: each flier needs its own shape. Reserve the long-serpent silhouette class for the apex.
- **Arcane nostalgia biome.** The natural home for variants: earlier-biome creatures reinterpreted with a lore reason, folk-tale motifs, swappable masks or looks. Variants must change mood and one part, not just colour; re-skins of old designs were called lazy in Gen 9.
- **Cosmic abyss.** Alien designs split players (Ultra Beasts scored 0.2 to 1% despite heavy push). Keep weirdness inviting: faces, chunky forms, a celestial motif carried by palette and a forehead or face mark.
- **The Hollow.** The origin and centre: home of the family mark shared by giants, legendaries and the Firstling. Quiet, mythic, restrained palette. Abstract concepts are allowed here only if they keep a face.

## 6. What makes a creature collectible and chase-worthy

**Variants.** A variant must change personality or mood and at least one part, not only the palette (time-of-day forms, habitat forms). Fewer strong forms beat many weak ones. Branching outcomes from one base are among the longest-lived hooks in the series, and part-based breeding is that hook built in.

**Shinies and rare finishes.** The change must be obvious at phone size, since shiny hunting in GO runs on visible difference. In clay, a rare finish (glaze, metallic, glitter, speckle) is the natural shiny. Earned in play only; never sold (CLAUDE.md policy rules).

**Rarity cues.** Silhouette class, scale, symmetry and finish signal "special" at a glance, but reveal hype fades without personality: Xerneas placed 4th of 69 at reveal and 37th of 721 years later. Rarity is felt through story (roaming, fleeing, a quest), not drop rates alone.

**Personality.** A one-line hook per species, two or three idle loops and one or two reactions, a flaw or quirk, and a companion mode where a creature follows the player. Let babies inherit a behaviour from a parent, so breeding changes how a creature acts as well as how it looks.

**Provenance.** The most valuable card in the hobby is valuable because of scarcity with a story and a named creator. Serial numbers, "bred by" and first-generation lineage give ClayPals creatures the same story without any paid randomness.

**Cards and merchandise.** Plush-friendly shapes (round, chunky, low detail) are the common thread of every merchandising success. Habitat-diorama portraits suit clay. Layered tiers of one creature (base, rare finish, lineage) work when earned. Seasonal cosmetics are a good lever only when cheap, optional and never battle-affecting: Unite's US$40 skins drew backlash. Staged reveals per biome build anticipation. Never take an owned creature away.

## 7. The pre-Meshy checklist

Every ClayPals creature must pass all of these before it goes to Meshy.

1. **One sentence.** Its concept fits in one sentence a child could say, with no pun needed.
2. **Anchor plus twist.** It is built on a recognisable animal or original mythic archetype, with exactly one twist. It is not an object with a face.
3. **Silhouette test.** As a flat black shape at 64 px it is recognisable and distinct from every other species in its biome.
4. **One signature feature** that a player would name it by, and no competing ornament.
5. **Palette.** One main colour, one accent, four at most, inside the biome palette.
6. **Face.** A clear, expressive face that shows a personality; the head carries character.
7. **Balance rule.** Cool designs have one soft or odd detail; cute designs have one tough detail. One deliberate handmade imperfection.
8. **Proportions** follow the assembler rules; nothing reads broken, bloated or uncanny.
9. **Every stage adds a new idea**, keeps one visible element from the earlier stage, and keeps the charm.
10. **Part test.** Its head, body, legs and tail each carry one readable idea and still look intentional when mixed with five random other species' parts.
11. **Role fit.** It meets the guidance for its role (section 4) and its biome (section 5).
12. **Behaviour.** It has a one-line personality, two or three idle loops, one or two reactions, and a job or gameplay reason to be used.
13. **Rarity fit.** If rare, it is special by silhouette class and story, and strong enough to justify its rarity; it is not a recolour of a sibling.
14. **Clean reading.** No accidental unfortunate shape, no grim or crude lore, no innuendo in its description.
15. **Originality.** It does not echo any existing creature's silhouette, name or signature feature, including Pokemon, and contains no real brands or IP.
16. **Plush test.** It would make a sensible plush or clay figure: chunky, low detail, stands up.
17. **Cull.** It beat other candidates for its slot. Gen 2 kept about 100 of several hundred concepts and Gen 5 drew about three times what shipped; draw many, keep few.
